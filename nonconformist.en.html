Essay 01

The Fence That Thinks

Property is often presented as a harmless legal form, as if ownership were merely a tidy way of naming who may use what. In reality, it organizes access to shelter, land, tools, and even attention by separating those who may decide from those who must ask. What looks like a neutral right is a social grammar of permission.

The fence is never only made of wood, law, or numbers. It is made of fear dressed up as order.

Once property is treated as sacred, dependence is translated into weakness and exclusion into responsibility. Those shut out from common resources are then told to call their lack of access freedom, provided they can still move, labor, and consume within the limits assigned to them.

A democratic society would begin elsewhere: from use before possession, from shared capacity before accumulated title, from life before abstract entitlement. The real question is not who owns the world, but who is allowed to inhabit it without pleading.

Aside: Gramsci, Raworth, Hannah Arendt

Lines of thought: David Graeber, Michel Foucault, Hannah Arendt

Essay 02

The Invisible Work of Keeping Things Alive

Care is treated as a private virtue, a family matter, or a sentimental supplement to the supposedly serious economy. That framing is already political, because it hides the labor that makes every other form of social activity possible. No institution stands on its own; each is carried by routines of maintenance, repair, feeding, listening, and endurance.

What sustains life is called secondary; what extracts value is called productive. This is not an oversight but a hierarchy.

The injustice lies not only in underpayment or overload, but in the way care is made to disappear once it succeeds. When needs are met, the work behind them is erased, as though breathing were a natural event rather than a maintained condition of the world we share.

A serious politics would not romanticize care, but redistribute it, honor its costs, and design institutions around vulnerability instead of pretending vulnerability is an exception. Human flourishing depends less on heroic achievement than on the ordinary labor that keeps people from falling apart.

Aside: Joan Tronto, Silvia Federici, Eva Illouz

Lines of thought: Karl Polanyi, Henri Lefebvre, Elinor Ostrom

Essay 03

The Tyranny of the Average

Normality is often invoked as if it were a calm center from which life can be judged. But every claim about what is normal already contains a map of who belongs, who deviates, and who must adapt in order to remain legible. The average is not innocent; it is a disciplinary fiction with social consequences.

The normal is not what is natural. It is what power has made familiar enough to stop noticing.

This is why so much exclusion appears polite. It does not always arrive with open force; it comes as the quiet demand to be like everyone else, where “everyone” means those already fitted to the dominant mold. Difference is then recoded as deficiency, and conformity is praised as maturity.

A freer society would not ask people to vanish into the median. It would treat plurality as a condition of collective life and measure institutions by their ability to make room for lives that do not resemble the template.

Aside: Foucault, Judith Butler, Ernst Bloch

Lines of thought: Silvia Federici, Joan Tronto, Nancy Fraser
