Essay 01

The Common We Keep Forgetting

Solidarity begins where the fiction of isolated self-sufficiency ends. It names the ordinary fact that no life is made alone, and that every freedom rests on hidden relations of care, labor, trust, and dependency. A society that denies this does not become more independent; it becomes more forgetful.

A market order prefers atomized subjects because atoms do not organize. What it calls individuality often means only vulnerability managed in private.

The political genius of solidarity lies in making interdependence visible without turning it into charity. It is neither pity nor fusion, but the recognition that injury is rarely private when its causes are structural. Where people are trained to compete for survival, solidarity restores the grammar of mutual obligation.

That is why solidarity is always contested at the level of imagination before it is contested at the level of institutions. It asks not merely who receives help, but what kind of world makes help necessary in the first place. The deeper task is not to perfect rescue, but to build conditions in which no one must be rescued alone.

Arendt, Gramsci, care ethics

Essay 02

The Fence Around the World

Property is often defended as if it were an innocent extension of personality, labor, or effort. But in practice it is a boundary-making power: it decides who may enter, who must ask, and who is kept outside before any conversation begins. It does not merely allocate things; it organizes social distance.

A fence is never only a line in space. It is a theory of the human being made visible.

Once ownership becomes the default lens, common life appears as loss, and enclosure appears as order. This reverses moral intuition: what was shared is recast as theft from the owner, while the original taking disappears behind legal formality. The result is a world in which scarcity is socially produced and then treated as proof that exclusion was necessary all along.

To criticize property is not to deny personal space or practical limits. It is to ask why so many vital conditions of life must be mediated by ownership at all, and why dependence is tolerated only when it can be monetized. The real question is whether a society can imagine stewardship without domination and use without seizure.

Raworth, Marx, enclosure critique

Essay 03

The Quiet Violence of the Average

Normality presents itself as common sense, but common sense is often the most durable form of discipline. It sorts lives before they are understood, rewarding those who fit the expected rhythm of productivity, composure, consumption, and speech. What appears neutral is usually already arranged.

The average is not innocent when it becomes a verdict. It can make a cage look like comfort.

The force of normality lies in its softness. It need not always prohibit directly, because it teaches people to self-correct in advance, to shrink their desires, and to mistrust whatever in them does not match the template. In this way, social control becomes psychological routine.

A free society would not abolish norms, but it would stop mistaking them for truth about human worth. It would treat variation as a source of intelligence rather than as a defect to be managed. The measure of emancipation is not how many people can conform, but how much life can appear without apology.

Foucault, Erving Goffman, normalization critique
