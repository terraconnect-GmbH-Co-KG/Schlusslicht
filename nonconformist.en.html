Essay 01

The Theft of Inner Bread

Attention is often described as a personal skill, as if concentration were only a matter of will. But the conditions of attention are social before they are psychological: noise, urgency, interruption, and permanent availability are organized into a regime that trains people to mistake fragmentation for freedom.

What is sold as connection often functions as dispersion. A mind that cannot linger is easier to steer.

The political meaning of attention is simple and severe: whoever controls the rhythm of perception also shapes the horizon of thought. Not every distraction is accidental; some are structural, built to keep reflection shallow enough to remain harmless.

A democratic culture would therefore begin not with louder speech, but with protected time, breathable silence, and forms of life that let thought arrive before demand. The right to attention is really the right to inhabit one’s own consciousness without constant annexation.

Lines of thought: Herbert Marcuse, Jonathan Crary, Simone Weil

Essay 02

Living at the Edge of Permission

Precarity is not merely uncertainty; it is uncertainty made governable. When life depends on renewal, approval, or continuous performance, the future stops being a shared field and becomes a narrow corridor guarded by administrative moods.

A person under permanent provisionality does not need to be openly commanded. The threat of falling is already enough.

This is why precarious conditions are so politically useful: they externalize discipline. People begin to police themselves, not because they are convinced, but because instability has been made into an atmosphere.

The deeper scandal is that insecurity is presented as realism, while protection is treated as an exception. Yet a society that normalizes fragility for many and insulation for a few has not discovered necessity; it has chosen hierarchy and called it prudence.

Lines of thought: Judith Butler, Pierre Bourdieu, Karl Polanyi

Essay 03

Tools That Teach Obedience

Technology is usually praised as if it floated above society, serving whichever purpose humans assign to it. But every technical system also carries a grammar of behavior: it suggests what is quick, what is visible, what is measurable, and eventually what counts as normal.

The machine does not only extend the hand. It instructs the hand in what to reach for.

That is why technical progress can coexist with shrinking freedom. A world may become more connected, more optimized, and more responsive while human initiative becomes thinner, because dependence is no longer felt as domination once it is wrapped in convenience.

The critical question is therefore not whether tools are useful, but what kind of subject they produce. If an instrument reorganizes desire, attention, and timing, then it is no longer merely a device; it is a form of governance in material disguise.

Lines of thought: Langdon Winner, Jacques Ellul, Bernard Stiegler
