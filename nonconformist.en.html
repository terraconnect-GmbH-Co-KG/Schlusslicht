Essay 01

Power Without a Face

Power is often imagined as something visible: a ruler, a boardroom, a decree, a hand on the lever. But its deeper form is quieter and more durable. It lives in routines, standards, metrics, and expectations that make certain actions feel natural and others unthinkable.

Power does not always need to forbid. It succeeds when it teaches people to participate in their own narrowing.

That is why domination so often appears benign at first. The most effective structures do not merely constrain bodies; they organize what counts as realism, competence, and common sense.

A democratic politics worthy of the name must therefore begin earlier than protest and later than obedience. It must ask who defines the field of the possible, who benefits from making that field look inevitable, and which forms of collective life have been trained to appear impossible before they are even tried.

Foucault, Gramsci, Hannah Arendt

Essay 02

Freedom After Compliance

Modern life often praises freedom while multiplying dependencies that make real refusal costly. A person may be formally unforced and still live inside a web of obligations, scarcity, and social punishment so dense that choice becomes a ritual rather than a power.

Freedom begins where obedience is no longer mistaken for realism. Without the material means to say no, choice is theatre.

This is why abstract liberties are not enough. A society can celebrate rights while depriving people of time, housing, security, and access to shared resources—the very conditions under which freedom becomes more than a word.

To defend freedom seriously is to defend the infrastructures of disobedience: time, space, education, and forms of mutual support that reduce coercion without promising perfection. Freedom is not the polished surface of individual autonomy; it is the collective capacity to live without being permanently managed by fear, need, or dependence.

Isaiah Berlin, Karl Polanyi, Amartya Sen

Essay 03

The Tyranny of More

Growth presents itself as neutral, even innocent, but it functions like a civilizational command. It demands expansion in production, consumption, extraction, and expectation, then calls that motion progress regardless of what it destroys on the way.

What grows without restraint does not prove vitality. It may only prove that no one is allowed to name enough.

The problem is not movement itself. The problem is a system that measures success by increase alone, while rendering repair, maintenance, and sufficiency secondary or invisible.

A different political imagination would treat flourishing as a question of balance, durability, and shared dignity rather than endless accumulation. That shift would not reject material life; it would refuse the fantasy that a society becomes richer simply because it becomes larger.

Kate Raworth, Ivan Illich, Erich Fromm
