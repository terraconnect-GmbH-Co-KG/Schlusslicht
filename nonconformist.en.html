Essay 01

Power Before the Order

Power is often mistaken for a visible hand, a face at the top, a command that can be pointed to and resisted. But the deeper form of power does not always announce itself as force; it arranges the field in which force becomes unnecessary. It organizes what appears realistic, what appears excessive, and what appears impossible.

The most effective power is the one that no longer needs to speak loudly. It teaches people to govern themselves as if obedience were common sense.

That is why critique cannot stop at personalities or isolated institutions. It must read the architecture of incentives, habits, and norms that makes domination feel ordinary. In this sense, the question is not only who decides, but what has already been decided in advance by the structure of the world itself.

A democratic politics worthy of the name would therefore begin with suspicion toward anything that presents itself as neutral while distributing advantage in silence. It would ask how consent is formed, how options are narrowed, and how dependency is disguised as freedom. The task is not merely to denounce rule, but to make the hidden grammar of rule legible.

Arendt, Foucault, Gramsci

Essay 02

Freedom Against Necessity

Freedom has been reduced too often to the vocabulary of selection: choose this, buy that, prefer one path over another. Yet choice alone does not make a life free if the available paths are prearranged by dependence, insecurity, and coercion. A person can be surrounded by options and still live under necessity.

Freedom begins where survival is no longer used as leverage. Where every refusal risks collapse, consent is only another name for constraint.

This is why a political theory of freedom must speak about housing, time, care, income, and public provision without embarrassment. What matters is not the abstract right to choose between branded cages, but the real capacity to say no. Freedom is not an interior mood; it is a social condition that either exists or does not.

To defend freedom, then, is to defend the material distance between a person and the threat that compels them. It is to widen the field of refusal until life is no longer organized around fear of loss. Only then can decision become genuine rather than theatrical.

Isaiah Berlin, Frantz Fanon, Nancy Fraser

Essay 03

The Common World We Keep Losing

Solidarity is often praised as a moral sentiment, as if it were simply kindness scaled upward. But its real meaning is more demanding: it names the recognition that no life is self-made and no freedom is privately produced. We live through one another whether institutions admit it or not.

A society organized against solidarity does not eliminate interdependence; it merely privatizes it and sells it back in fragments. The result is loneliness with invoices.

This is why solidarity cannot be reduced to charity, and cannot be delegated to moments of crisis. It requires durable forms of mutual support that survive competition, suspicion, and the endless conversion of need into market opportunity. Without such forms, the public sphere becomes a marketplace of isolated emergencies.

To think politically about solidarity is to refuse the fiction that dignity must be earned in solitude. It is to rebuild the conditions under which people can rely on each other without humiliation. The shared world is not what remains after self-interest; it is what makes any life human at all.

Hannah Arendt, Karl Polanyi, Silvia Federici
