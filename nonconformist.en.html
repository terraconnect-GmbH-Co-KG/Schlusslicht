Essay 01

The Hidden Infrastructure of Life

Every society depends on repair, feeding, cleaning, listening, nursing, and holding things together, yet these forms of labor are usually treated as if they were natural rather than political. That concealment is not an accident; it is a stable way of organizing whose work counts as real and whose work is made invisible.

What is called productivity often rests on an unpaid ground of exhaustion. The system applauds the building and forgets the keeping.

Care exposes a deep contradiction: the more a society celebrates autonomy, the more it relies on dependency it refuses to name. The fantasy of the self-made individual survives only because others absorb the costs of fragility, repetition, and vulnerability.

A different politics would begin from maintenance as a shared public concern, not a private burden assigned by habit, gender, or class. The central question is not how to make life look heroic, but how to organize the ordinary labor that makes life possible in the first place.

Arendt, feminist care ethics, reproduction as political infrastructure

Essay 02

The Moral Theater of Winners

Meritocracy presents social rank as the transparent outcome of effort, intelligence, and discipline. In practice, it transforms inherited advantage into a story of personal deserving, while turning structural barriers into supposed evidence of inferiority.

The cruel elegance of merit is that it makes domination feel fair. When privilege learns to speak the language of achievement, guilt disappears and hierarchy acquires a halo.

This ideology is especially effective because it does not need open brutality to function. It only needs comparison, competition, and the constant moral sorting of lives into the successful and the expendable.

An equal society would weaken the moral mythology around rank and replace it with a sober account of interdependence, inheritance, and chance. The point is not to deny effort, but to stop mistaking social victory for ethical superiority.

Bourdieu, Rawls, ideology critique

Essay 03

The Religion of More

Growth is often treated as the universal answer to stagnation, insecurity, and crisis. But a system that must always expand to justify itself eventually turns life into an auxiliary of accumulation, even when that expansion corrodes the conditions of living.

More is not a horizon; it is a trap when it cannot stop asking for itself. A society that worships growth begins to call exhaustion momentum and ruin success.

The deeper problem is not size alone, but the political imagination that measures vitality only through accumulation. That logic makes ecological limits look like obstacles rather than facts, and mutual dependence look like weakness rather than design.

A post-growth perspective would not romanticize scarcity, but it would reject the fiction that endless expansion can serve finite beings on a finite planet. The real question is how to build enough, distribute well, and stop confusing appetite with necessity.

Doughnut economics, Illich, ecological political economy
