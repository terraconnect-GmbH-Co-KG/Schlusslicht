Essay 01

The Fence That Calls Itself Nature

Property is usually presented as a simple answer to a practical question: who may use what, and under which conditions? But this language hides a deeper move. Once ownership is treated as natural, the social world is reorganized around permission, and permission is always a form of power.

A fence does not merely mark a limit. It teaches a society to mistake separation for legitimacy.

The language of ownership also changes the moral grammar of life. What was once shared becomes managed scarcity, and what was once dependence becomes a private burden. In that shift, exclusion is no longer experienced as a political choice but as common sense.

A critical philosophy of property does not deny the need for rules; it asks who gets to define them and whose life becomes thinkable inside them. The point is not to abolish all boundaries, but to stop treating inherited boundaries as if they were the world itself. A democratic society begins when shared conditions are recognized as the starting point, not the leftover.

Hannah Arendt, Karl Marx, David Graeber

Essay 02

When Work Becomes a Moral Test

Work is often praised as if it were identical with dignity. Yet under capitalist conditions, work is rarely just activity; it is a filter that decides whose time counts, whose effort is visible, and whose exhaustion can be ignored. The ideology of work turns dependence into shame and obedience into virtue.

The cruelest trick is simple: necessity is renamed freedom. Then the cage speaks in the voice of self-fulfillment.

This is why so many people are asked to prove their value through productivity even when the social world depends on care, maintenance, and invisible repetition. The system honors the outputs it can price and forgets the labor it cannot easily celebrate. What matters most is not always what is paid most.

A different politics would begin by separating social worth from market performance. It would treat time, health, rest, and participation as conditions of life rather than rewards for productivity. In that view, work is no longer a tribunal of merit but one human activity among others, limited by the needs of a shared world.

Karl Marx, Hannah Arendt, Silvia Federici

Essay 03

Voting Without Power Is Not Rule

Democracy is often reduced to the ceremony of voting, as if a brief act of consent could substitute for genuine self-government. But a system does not become democratic because it allows periodic approval; it becomes democratic when people can revise the structures that govern their lives. Without that, participation is only a ritual of legitimacy.

Consent given once in a while is not sovereignty. It is often just the polite administration of exclusion.

The deeper problem is that modern institutions can absorb public voice without surrendering public control. Debate is invited, but binding power remains elsewhere. Citizens are addressed as audiences, not authors; they are consulted, not empowered.

A serious democratic imagination would therefore focus less on symbolic inclusion and more on durable access to decision-making. It would ask who sets the agenda, who can intervene, and who can actually change the rules. Democracy is not the right to applaud the system at intervals; it is the capacity to reshape it while living inside it.

Hannah Arendt, Antonio Gramsci, Claude Lefort
