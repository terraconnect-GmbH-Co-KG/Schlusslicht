Essay 01

The Captured Mind

Attention is often treated as a personal skill, a matter of discipline, temperament, or better habits. That view is convenient because it hides the deeper fact that attention is now organized by environments built to interrupt, accelerate, and monetize perception. What appears as an individual weakness is frequently a social design.

The deepest theft is not time but orientation. A mind that cannot settle cannot judge.

Under such conditions, distraction is not an accident but a mode of governance. It keeps consciousness moving too quickly to form durable comparisons, shared memory, or sustained refusal. A fragmented public is easier to manage than a thinking one.

The political issue, then, is not only what people see, but what they can hold together long enough to understand. A liberated attention would not mean permanent concentration; it would mean the recovered ability to pause, to distinguish urgency from importance, and to decide what deserves a life. That is a democratic question before it is a psychological one.

Frantz Fanon, Walter Benjamin, attention economy

Essay 02

The Future on Lease

Debt is usually presented as a neutral contract between equals, yet its social reality is far more asymmetric. It binds the present to a promised future under terms set in the past, and it does so in language that makes compulsion sound like responsibility. In that sense, debt is not only financial; it is a form of temporal command.

Debt teaches the borrower to narrate necessity as virtue. That is how rule becomes self-policing.

The moral force of debt is one of its sharpest instruments. It does not merely demand repayment; it reshapes desire, narrows risk, and trains people to accept scarcity as personal failure instead of collective arrangement. The result is a citizenry that apologizes for being constrained.

A critical view of debt does not deny obligations. It asks who has the power to define them, who benefits from their permanence, and why entire lives should be organized around servicing yesterday's decisions. A humane society would treat the future as a shared horizon, not as collateral.

David Graeber, Michel Foucault, Karl Polanyi

Essay 03

The Violence of the Average

Normality is often invoked as if it were a description of reality, but it functions more like a silent verdict. It divides acceptable from suspect, healthy from deviant, productive from excessive, without admitting that these lines are drawn by institutions, routines, and expectations. What is called normal is usually what has become familiar to power.

The average is not innocent. It is a border that pretends to be a mirror.

This is why normality can be so effective without appearing coercive. It does not always need punishment; it works through embarrassment, aspiration, and the fear of standing out. Many forms of conformity begin not in command but in the wish to remain legible.

A serious critique of normality must therefore ask what kinds of lives are made difficult by its standards of legibility. The point is not to celebrate eccentricity for its own sake, but to defend the right to differ without being treated as incomplete. Every social order that claims to be self-evident should be suspected of hiding its exclusions in plain sight.

Hannah Arendt, Michel Foucault, Antonio Gramsci
